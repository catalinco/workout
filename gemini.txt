why there is no lowbar squat and no overhead press?

The absence of the low bar squat and the overhead press (specifically the strict "Olympic Press") usually comes down to whether you are looking at Olympic Weightlifting as a sport or a specific training philosophy.

Here is why they are missing from certain competitive and training environments:
1. The Olympic Games (Weightlifting)

If you are asking why you don't see these in the Olympics, it's because of a major historical split in strength sports:

    The Overhead Press was removed in 1972: Until the 1972 Munich Games, the "Clean and Press" was one of the three Olympic lifts (alongside the Snatch and Clean & Jerk). It was removed because judging became a nightmare. Lifters would lean back so far to use their chest muscles that it became a "standing bench press," making it dangerous for the spine and impossible for judges to determine what was a "clean" vertical press.
    Low Bar Squat isn't a competition lift: The Olympics features Weightlifting, not Powerlifting. Powerlifting (which includes the low bar squat, bench press, and deadlift) is a separate sport that is not currently in the Olympic Games.

2. Olympic Weightlifting Training

Even in training, Olympic lifters almost never use the low bar squat.

    Torso Angle: The low bar squat requires you to lean forward to keep the bar over your mid-foot. However, to catch a heavy Snatch or Clean, you need a perfectly vertical torso.
    Transferability: High bar squats and front squats mimic the "catch" position of Olympic lifts much better. Low bar squats build massive posterior chain strength (hips/back), but they don't help an Olympic lifter stay upright under a 400lb barbell.

3. Modern "Functional" Fitness (CrossFit & Apps)

In many 2026 fitness trends and "functional" apps (like Nike Training Club or Peloton), these lifts are often replaced by variations:

    Safety & Learning Curve: The low bar squat is highly technical and can be hard on the shoulders and wrists for beginners. Many programs favor the High Bar Squat or Goblet Squat because they are easier to teach and safer for general fitness.
    The "Push Press" Takeover: In programs like CrossFit, the strict overhead press is often sidelined in favor of the Push Press or Jerk. These allow you to move more weight and involve the whole body, which fits the "high-intensity" goals of those programs better than the strict isolation of a military press.

However, in the context of Iaido (the art of drawing the sword), these two specific lifts have a complicated relationship with the technique. Here is how you should think about including them in your training:
1. The Low Bar Squat vs. Iaido Posture

The low bar squat is the king of hip-dominant strength, but it has one major drawback for swordsmanship: The Lean.

    The Conflict: Low bar squats require you to lean your torso forward to keep the bar over your midfoot. In Iaido, a forward lean is considered a major technical flaw. Whether you are in seiza (kneeling), tatehiza (half-seated), or standing, your spine must remain perfectly vertical to allow for the rapid rotation of the hips and a clean draw (nukitsuke).
    The Recommendation: Don't drop squats, but consider High Bar or Front Squats. These variations force a vertical torso and more "quad-dominant" stability, which carries over directly to the deep lunges and explosive standing movements in Iaido.
    The Benefit: Your 150kg squat means your legs won't get tired during long seminars. That "shaking leg" fatigue that beginners feel? You’ve already beaten it.

2. The Overhead Press (OHP) and Sword Mechanics

The OHP is a "pushing" movement, while a sword cut is essentially a "pulling" movement.

    The Conflict: In a classic downward cut (kirioroshi), you aren't pushing the sword; you are using your latissimus dorsi and core to pull the blade through the target. Over-relying on the deltoids (the primary OHP muscles) can actually make your cuts "choppy" and slow.
    The Benefit: Where the OHP shines for Iaido is Shoulder Health and Stability. Holding a sword at arm's length or in a high guard (jodan-no-kamae) puts significant isometric stress on the shoulders. A 75kg OHP ensures your stabilizers are bulletproof, protecting you from the "swordsman’s elbow" and rotator cuff issues common in the art.

3. The "Tension Trap"

The biggest danger for a strong lifter in Iaido is stiffness. Iaido requires "relaxed power." If you "death-grip" the sword with 75kg-presser strength, your technique will be stiff, and your blade speed will actually decrease.

    Expert Tip: In 2026, many high-level martial arts S&C coaches recommend the "80% Rule." Continue your heavy lifts to maintain your "strength floor," but never lift so heavy that your muscles are too sore or tight to move fluidly in the dojo the next day.

Here are the three specific modifications you need for Tango:
1. The "Dissociation" Problem

In Tango, your upper body often moves independently of your lower body (e.g., in ochos). Powerlifters are trained to move the torso and hips as a single, rigid unit to protect the spine.

    The Issue: Your core might be too stiff. If you can't rotate your ribcage while keeping your hips facing forward, your Tango will look "blocky" and your partner will feel like they are dancing with a statue.
    The Fix: Add thoracic mobility and rotational core work. Movements like Pallof Presses or Windmills will help you keep that 75kg OHP strength while regaining the ability to twist.

2. Single-Leg Stability & "The Axis"

A 150kg squat is a "bilateral" feat (two legs). Tango is almost entirely "unilateral" (one leg). Most of the time, you are balancing your entire weight—plus some of your partner's—on a single, slightly bent leg.

    The Issue: You can have a massive squat and still have "leaky" ankles or weak glute medius muscles, causing you to lose your "axis" (balance) during pivots.
    The Fix: You need Single-Leg strength. I recommend adding Bulgarian Split Squats or Single-Leg RDLs. This ensures that the 150kg strength translates into a rock-solid, unshakeable balance on the dance floor.

3. The "Chest-to-Chest" Connection

In Tango, the "Embrace" (Abrazo) requires a sensitive but firm connection.

    The Issue: Heavy OHP and Squats can lead to tight pecs and "forward-rounded" shoulders. In Tango, you need a "proud" chest and shoulders that stay down and back, away from your ears.
    The Fix: Prioritize Face Pulls and Active Lat Stretches. This counters the "tightness" of the heavy press and ensures your embrace feels inviting and stable rather than stiff and aggressive.

1. The Olympic Games (Weightlifting)

If you are asking why you don't see these in the Olympics, it's because of a major historical split in strength sports:

    The Overhead Press was removed in 1972: Until the 1972 Munich Games, the "Clean and Press" was one of the three Olympic lifts (alongside the Snatch and Clean & Jerk). It was removed because judging became a nightmare. Lifters would lean back so far to use their chest muscles that it became a "standing bench press," making it dangerous for the spine and impossible for judges to determine what was a "clean" vertical press.

    Low Bar Squat isn't a competition lift: The Olympics features Weightlifting, not Powerlifting. Powerlifting (which includes the low bar squat, bench press, and deadlift) is a separate sport that is not currently in the Olympic Games.

2. Olympic Weightlifting Training

Even in training, Olympic lifters almost never use the low bar squat.

    Torso Angle: The low bar squat requires you to lean forward to keep the bar over your mid-foot. However, to catch a heavy Snatch or Clean, you need a perfectly vertical torso.

    Transferability: High bar squats and front squats mimic the "catch" position of Olympic lifts much better. Low bar squats build massive posterior chain strength (hips/back), but they don't help an Olympic lifter stay upright under a 400lb barbell.

3. Modern "Functional" Fitness (CrossFit & Apps)

In many 2026 fitness trends and "functional" apps (like Nike Training Club or Peloton), these lifts are often replaced by variations:

    Safety & Learning Curve: The low bar squat is highly technical and can be hard on the shoulders and wrists for beginners. Many programs favor the High Bar Squat or Goblet Squat because they are easier to teach and safer for general fitness.

    The "Push Press" Takeover: In programs like CrossFit, the strict overhead press is often sidelined in favor of the Push Press or Jerk. # Fatigue Management & Weight Adjustment Guide

In this program, your performance in the Dojo and on the Dance Floor is the priority. Use these three "Audibles" if you are feeling fatigued.

## 1. The "RPE" Adjustment (Daily)
Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale of 1-10 (10 being max effort).
* **Target:** RPE 8 (You should finish every set feeling like you could have done 2 more reps).
* **The Rule:** If the weight feels like an RPE 9 or 10 during your first set, **reduce the weight by 5-10% immediately.** It is better to move a lighter weight with "Tango-like" control than to grind out a heavy rep with "broken" form.

## 2. The "Tango/Iaido" Pre-Check
If you have a session in the evening and feel particularly stiff:
* **Cut the Sets, Not the Weight:** Instead of 3 sets of Squats, do **2 sets**. This maintains your strength peak but reduces the "Volume Fatigue" that causes heavy legs during dancing.
* **Focus on the "Concentric":** Move the weight up quickly, but don't go too slow on the way down (eccentric). Slow eccentrics cause the most muscle soreness (DOMS).

## 3. The "Emergency" Scale-Back
If you are entering Week 4 or 5 and find your Iaido forms are becoming "sloppy" or your balance is off:
* **Swap Compound for Isolation:** Swap the **Low Bar Squat** for **Leg Press** for one session. This removes the tax on your lower back and CNS while still keeping your legs strong.
* **Eliminate the "Finisher":** Skip the 6th exercise (Wrist Curls, Lateral Lunges, or Leg Raises) to save 15 minutes of energy.

If you experience these, take an extra rest day or move directly to the **Week 6 Deload**:
1.  **Grip Failure:** You can't hold the Katana/Bokken as firmly as usual.
